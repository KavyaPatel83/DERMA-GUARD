# How skin lesions are diagnosed and treated

## Clinical examination and dermoscopy
A dermatologist first takes a history, including how long the lesion has been present and whether it has changed, then examines the whole skin. Dermoscopy uses a handheld magnifier with polarised light to see pigment patterns and vessels beneath the surface, and improves diagnostic accuracy over the naked eye. Total body photography and digital dermoscopy monitoring can track people with many moles.

## Biopsy
A biopsy is the definitive test. The common types are a shave or punch biopsy for suspected non-melanoma skin cancer, and a complete excisional biopsy with narrow margins for suspected melanoma. The tissue is examined by a pathologist, who reports the diagnosis and, for melanoma, the thickness (Breslow depth), which guides further treatment. Results usually take one to two weeks.

## Treatment options
Surgical excision removes the lesion with a margin of normal skin. Mohs micrographic surgery removes cancer layer by layer with immediate microscopic checking and is preferred for BCC and SCC in cosmetically or functionally sensitive sites such as the face. Cryotherapy freezes superficial lesions such as actinic keratoses. Curettage and electrodesiccation treat small low-risk cancers. Topical treatments such as imiquimod and fluorouracil treat superficial BCC and actinic keratosis. Photodynamic therapy uses a light-activated medicine. Radiotherapy suits patients who cannot have surgery. Advanced melanoma is treated with immunotherapy or targeted therapy under an oncology team.

## Follow-up
After treatment of a skin cancer, regular skin checks are recommended because the risk of a new skin cancer is higher. Frequency depends on the type and stage, commonly every three to twelve months at first. Patients should continue monthly self-examination and strict sun protection.

## What an AI tool can and cannot do
Computer-aided image analysis can help prioritise which lesions need specialist attention, but it cannot replace biopsy and histopathology. Its output is a probability estimate based on patterns in training data, and it may be wrong, especially for rare lesion types, unusual body sites, poor photographs, and skin tones that were under-represented in the training data.
