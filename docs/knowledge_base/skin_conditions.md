# Skin conditions covered by DERMA-GUARD

## Basal cell carcinoma (BCC)
Basal cell carcinoma is the most common skin cancer. It arises from the basal cells in the deepest layer of the epidermis and is strongly linked to cumulative sun exposure. It typically appears on the face, nose, ears, neck and other sun-exposed sites as a pearly or waxy bump, a flat flesh-coloured or brown scar-like patch, or a sore that bleeds, crusts and does not heal within a few weeks. Small visible blood vessels on the surface are common. BCC grows slowly and very rarely spreads to other organs, but it can destroy surrounding skin, cartilage and bone if left untreated. Treatment is usually surgical excision or Mohs micrographic surgery; superficial lesions may be treated with topical imiquimod, curettage, cryotherapy or photodynamic therapy. Prognosis is excellent when treated, but people who have had one BCC are at higher risk of another.

## Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)
Squamous cell carcinoma is the second most common skin cancer and develops from keratinocytes in the upper layers of the epidermis. It often appears as a firm red nodule, a scaly or crusted flat sore, or a rough patch that may bleed, on sun-exposed skin such as the face, lips, ears, scalp, hands and forearms. It can arise from actinic keratosis. Unlike BCC, SCC has a real potential to spread to lymph nodes and distant organs, especially when large, deep, on the lip or ear, or in immunosuppressed people. Standard treatment is surgical excision or Mohs surgery, sometimes with radiotherapy. Early treatment gives a high cure rate. Any non-healing scaly or bleeding lesion should be examined promptly.

## Melanoma (MEL)
Melanoma is the most dangerous skin cancer because it can spread early. It arises from melanocytes and may develop in an existing mole or appear as a new dark spot. The ABCDE rule describes warning features: Asymmetry, irregular Border, uneven Colour, Diameter over about 6 millimetres, and Evolution, meaning change in size, shape, colour, or new symptoms such as itching or bleeding. Nodular melanomas can be raised, firm and uniformly dark or even pink. Melanoma found early, when thin, is usually cured by wide local excision. Thicker melanomas may need lymph node assessment and systemic therapy such as immunotherapy. Any suspected melanoma needs urgent specialist assessment and, usually, excision biopsy.

## Actinic keratosis (ACK)
Actinic keratosis is a rough, dry, scaly patch caused by long-term ultraviolet exposure. It is considered pre-malignant: a small proportion of lesions can progress to squamous cell carcinoma, and it is a marker of significant sun damage. Lesions are often easier to feel than to see, may itch, sting or burn, and occur on the face, scalp, ears, hands and forearms. Treatment includes cryotherapy (liquid nitrogen), topical fluorouracil, imiquimod, diclofenac, or photodynamic therapy. Lesions that become thick, painful, bleed or grow quickly should be biopsied to exclude SCC.

## Melanocytic nevus (NEV)
A melanocytic nevus is a common mole made of clusters of pigment cells. Most are harmless. Typical moles are symmetrical, have a smooth even border, one uniform colour and are smaller than about 6 millimetres. Most people have 10 to 40 moles. Having many moles, or atypical moles, increases melanoma risk, so periodic skin checks are recommended. A mole that changes, bleeds, itches, or looks different from the others (the "ugly duckling" sign) should be checked by a clinician. Benign moles do not require removal unless they are suspicious or bothersome.

## Seborrheic keratosis (SEK)
Seborrheic keratosis is a very common benign skin growth that increases with age. It appears as a waxy, wart-like, "stuck-on" tan, brown or black plaque with a well-defined edge, often on the trunk, face and back. It is harmless and does not turn into cancer, but it can sometimes be confused with melanoma. Removal, by cryotherapy or curettage, is only for cosmetic reasons, irritation, or when the diagnosis is uncertain.
